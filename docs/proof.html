The proof as a sequence of simpler questions

How a weighted sampling problem becomes one counting problem

The proof starts with arbitrary values and signed weights. It first shows what a worst case must look like, then turns that worst case into the number of marked items drawn without replacement. An exact relation to a smaller population finishes the bound.

1

Which population values can be worst?

From an interval to zeros and ones

First pad the weights with zeros to \(N\) coordinates and subtract their average. This removes the part of the weights that cannot affect a centered statistic and gives the exact identity

\[ \rho_N(w)=\frac{N}{N-1}\lVert w^\circ\rVert_2^2. \]

With \(\sigma_N^2=N^{-1}\sum_j(X_j-\bar X_N)^2\), this is the coefficient appearing in the exact identity \(\operatorname{Var}(T_w)=\rho_N(w)\sigma_N^2\). The MGF theorem is designed to retain this same coefficient globally in \(t\), not only to second order at the origin.

Next rescale every population value from \([a,b]\) to \([0,1]\). The exponential average is convex in each population coordinate, so its maximum over this cube occurs at a corner. A corner contains only zeros and ones. Once the number \(K\) of ones is fixed, a random permutation is equivalent to choosing a uniformly random \(K\)-element subset.

Mathematics · exact chain

interval cube→ binary vertex→ K-subset→ centered slice MGF

Nothing is approximated in these bridges, and the weights may still be positive or negative.

Which weight pattern can be worst?

Why two weight values are enough

Now fix two features of the padded weight vector: its coordinates sum to zero and its squared length is fixed. A maximizing vector exists. The key question is whether that maximizer could need many different coordinate values.

Focus on any three coordinates while holding their sum and squared length fixed. Those three points move on a circle. The first- and second-order conditions, together with a repeated-Rolle/Hermite argument, rule out a maximum with three distinct values. Repeating this for every triple shows that a global maximizer can be chosen with at most two weight values.

Section 3 · cited conclusion used by the paper

\[ \max_{\substack{\mathbf 1^\top y=0\\ \lVert y\rVert_2^2=r^2}} \operatorname{MGF}_{N,K}(y) \quad\text{is attained by a vector with at most two values.} \]

3

The remaining counting problem

Relating the count to a smaller population

Suppose \(K\) of the \(N\) population items are marked and \(m\) items are drawn without replacement. Here \(m\) is the draw size of the auxiliary hypergeometric problem, not the original sample size \(n\) in \(T_w\). Let \(H_{N,K,m}\) count how many marked items are drawn. After subtracting its mean \(Km/N\), its MGF \(G_{N,K,m}\) satisfies the exact derivative identity

\[ G'_{N,K,m}(t) =2v_{N,K,m}e^{r_{N,K,m}t}\sinh(t/2) G_{N-2,K-1,m-1}(t), \] \[ v_{N,K,m}=\frac{K(N-K)m(N-m)}{N^2(N-1)}. \]

The smaller MGF on the right uses a population of size \(N-2\), with one fewer marked item and one fewer draw. Crucially, the multiplier \(v_{N,K,m}\) is exactly the variance of the original count. This is why the proof retains the precise sample-size factor \(m(N-m)/(N-1)\).

The derivative comparison also uses \(2\sinh(t/2)/t\le e^{t^2/24}\). The paper proves this elementary estimate from the power series, and Lean checks the same step through sinh_le_mul_exp_sq_div_six and two_sinh_div_le_exp_sq_div_twentyFour.

Where even and odd sizes differ

Exact balance exists only when \(N\) is even

Even \(N\)

The constant-\(1\) bound is already best possible. The exactly balanced choice \(K=N/2\) exists, and the behavior as \(t\) approaches zero forces the constant to be at least \(1\).

Odd \(N\)

The integer \(N/2\) does not exist. The closest possible split identifies a smaller candidate constant, but proving it for every draw size requires one additional comparison in the most balanced case.

5

Why the constant cannot be improved

Return from the count to the main theorem

To ask for the smallest possible constant, maximize the normalized log MGF over every number \(K\) of marked items, every draw size \(m\), and every nonzero real \(t\). The proved upper bound matches a lower-bound example: near \(t=0\) when \(N\) is even, and at an explicit nonzero \(t\) when \(N\) is odd.

\[ \kappa_N^* = \max_{1\le m\le N-1}\max_{0\le K\le N} \sup_{t\ne0} \frac{8(N-1)}{m(N-m)t^2}\log G_{N,K,m}(t) =\kappa_N. \]

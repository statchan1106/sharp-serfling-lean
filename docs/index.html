Sampling without replacement, explained and verified

A sharp variance-scale refinement of Serfling’s inequality

Seongchan Lee · Ilmun Kim

This page explains a simple question: how much more concentrated is an average when we sample without replacement? The answer uses the exact finite-population variance factor, extends to signed weighted sums, and is checked in Lean 4.

Start with the idea See the main result

In one sentence

As the sample grows toward the whole population, randomness disappears. The theorem keeps exactly this sample-size improvement and finds the smallest population-size correction that works in every case.

The question before the formulas

Sampling without replacement should become easier to predict

If an item cannot be drawn twice, every observation tells us something about what remains. When all \(N\) items are observed, the sample mean is the population mean and there is no randomness left.

Small sample

Some uncertainty remains

The sample mean can still move because most of the population has not been seen.

Large sample

Less can vary

The exact variance shrinks by the factor \((N-n)/(N-1)\).

Whole population

The error is zero

At \(n=N\), the sample mean equals the population mean exactly.

\[ \begin{aligned} \operatorname{Var}(\bar X_{S_n}) &=\frac{\sigma_N^2}{n}\frac{N-n}{N-1},\\ \sigma_N^2&=\frac1N\sum_{j=1}^N(X_j-\bar X_N)^2. \end{aligned} \]

The main result: the same factor that appears in the exact variance also remains valid in an exponential concentration bound. The paper then identifies the smallest additional correction depending only on \(N\).

The main result

The weighted bound, in plain language

Start with \(N\) fixed numbers between \(a\) and \(b\), reveal them in a uniformly random order, and attach any positive or negative weights to the first \(n\) observations. The quantity \(T_w\) is the resulting weighted error after subtracting the population mean.

An MGF, short for moment generating function, is the exponential average \(\mathbb E[e^{tT_w}]\). Controlling it for every real \(t\) gives upper, lower, and two-sided probability bounds. The theorem says:

\[ \log \mathbb E e^{tT_w} \le \frac{\kappa_N}{8}\rho_N(w)(b-a)^2t^2. \] \[ \rho_N(w)=\frac{N\sum_i w_i^2-(\sum_i w_i)^2}{N-1}. \]
\(T_w\)

The weighted deviation we want to control.

\(\rho_N(w)\)

The exact variance contribution of the weights: \(\operatorname{Var}(T_w)=\rho_N(w)\sigma_N^2\). Adding the same constant to every padded weight does not change it.

\(\kappa_N\)

The smallest extra correction depending only on the population size \(N\).

“Sharp” means best possible. The same development proves that no smaller constant depending only on \(N\) can work simultaneously for every bounded population, sample size, weight vector, and real value of \(t\).

How the proof becomes manageable

Four ideas to keep in mind

The original statement allows arbitrary bounded values and signed weights. The proof repeatedly replaces this large problem by a smaller one without losing the exact variance factor.

  1. 1

    Only the two endpoints can be worst

    After rescaling \([a,b]\) to \([0,1]\), convexity shows that it is enough to consider populations made only of zeros and ones.

  2. 2

    The worst weight pattern uses two values

    Among centered weights of a fixed size, a maximizing pattern can be chosen with at most two distinct values. This is the main geometric reduction.

  3. 3

    The problem becomes counting marked items

    With a zero-one population and two weight levels, the statistic is a rescaled hypergeometric count: the number of marked items in a sample drawn without replacement.

  4. 4

    Compare with a smaller counting problem

    An exact derivative identity reduces \((N,K,m)\) to \((N-2,K-1,m-1)\). The last step differs according to whether \(N\) can be split into two equal halves.

Where the present paper begins. The binary-population and two-level reductions were introduced in A Sharper Hoeffding Bound for Weighted Sums of Exchangeable Random Variables; its Lean project gives the earlier exchangeable setting. The present paper starts from those reductions and proves the centered hypergeometric recursion and its sharp variance-scale constant. This repository also verifies the cited reductions internally.

The best population-size correction

Why even and odd population sizes differ

The most difficult zero-one population is as balanced as possible. An exact half-and-half split exists when \(N\) is even, but not when \(N\) is odd. That small arithmetic difference changes the best uniform constant.

\[ \kappa_N= \begin{cases} 1, & N \text{ even},\\[4pt] \displaystyle \frac{2}{N\log((N+1)/(N-1))}, & N \text{ odd}. \end{cases} \]

When \(N\) is even

A population with exactly \(N/2\) zeros and \(N/2\) ones exists. Behavior near \(t=0\) already forces the best constant to be \(1\).

When \(N\) is odd

Exact balance is impossible. The closest split gives a constant strictly below \(1\), attained at an explicit nonzero value of \(t\).

What readers can take away

Consequences, scope, and formal trust

Variance-scale Serfling correction

With equal weights, \(T_w\) is the difference between the sample mean and the population mean. The probability bounds retain the exact sample-size factor \((N-n)/(N-1)\).

Weighted exchangeable transfer

If relabeling the coordinates does not change their joint distribution, the same analysis controls signed weighted contrasts and identifies the smallest uniform constant.

Kernel-checked scope

MGF

The exponential average \(\mathbb E[e^{tZ}]\). A quadratic upper bound on its logarithm yields concentration inequalities.

Hypergeometric count

The number of marked items found when a fixed-size sample is drawn without replacement.
